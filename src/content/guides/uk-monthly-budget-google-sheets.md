## Who this is for

This is for UK households — solo or sharing with a partner — who want a simple monthly plan in **Google Sheets**, not Excel, and not a US template full of 401(k) and “utilities (avg)” that do not match British bills.

You want GBP, categories that sound like your life (council tax, TV licence, energy), and a living file you can open on a laptop or phone.

If you also need to track self-employment income for HMRC, that is a separate job from a household budget. A Self Assessment / SA103 tracker is for tax records; this article is for planning household cash flow.

---

## Budget sheet vs banking app

Banking apps such as Monzo or Starling are excellent at showing **what already left the account**. A budget spreadsheet is the **plan**: what you meant to spend, what you actually spent, and what is left before payday.

Use both:

- The app (or statements) for truth about transactions
- The sheet for categories, planned amounts, sinking funds, and the weekly check-in

A template will not fix overspending on its own. The weekly habit of comparing planned vs actual is what makes the sheet useful.

For free government-backed guidance and a separate online planner, see [MoneyHelper’s budget planner](https://www.moneyhelper.org.uk/en/everyday-money/budgeting/budget-planner). A Google Sheet sits beside that kind of tool when you want a file you own and can customise.

## Make your own copy

Work in **your** Google Drive so edits stay yours.

Typical flow if you buy or download a template:

1. Open the PDF you receive from the shop (it contains the Google Sheets link)
2. Open the sheet in Google Sheets
3. Use **File → Make a copy**
4. Rename it (for example `Household budget 2026`) and save it in a Drive folder you will actually open

If you build your own from scratch, create a blank Sheet and add the columns or tabs below — same idea, more setup time.

Do not edit the original shared file if the link is view-only; always copy first.

## UK categories that matter

Start with categories that match how UK households actually pay bills. Rename anything that does not fit; delete what you never use.

**Housing and council**

- Rent or mortgage
- Council tax (often billed over **10 months**, April to January — plan for the months it is due)
- Home insurance (if paid monthly or as a sinking fund)

**Utilities and home**

- Gas and electricity (or a dual-fuel bill)
- Water
- Broadband
- Mobile phones
- TV licence

**Everyday living**

- Groceries and household shopping
- Transport (fuel, railcards, buses, parking)
- Childcare or school-related costs
- Subscriptions (streaming, cloud storage, memberships)

**Money you already owe**

- Debt minimum payments (credit cards, loans, overdraft plan)
- Any agreed extra you are putting towards balances

**Future you**

- Emergency buffer / rainy-day transfer
- Sinking funds (see below)
- Longer-term savings

Keep the list short enough that you will update it. Ten clear categories beat thirty abandoned ones.

## Start with take-home pay

Budget from **money that actually hits your account**, not the gross salary on a payslip.

List income lines clearly, for example:

- Pay after tax and National Insurance (and student loan if it is deducted)
- Partner’s take-home (if you share a budget)
- Regular side income you can rely on
- Benefits or other regular credits you treat as income

If your pay varies (shifts, freelance, commission), use a **cautious** month as the plan — a typical quieter month — and treat stronger months as bonus top-ups to savings or debt, not as a new permanent lifestyle floor.

Add the income total at the top of the month so every planned pound has a job, or sits in “unallocated” until you choose one.

## Planned vs actual

Fill **planned** amounts first — before the month runs away.

Then log **actual** spend. Two workable methods:

1. **Transaction log** — paste or type lines from the bank app into an Expenses tab, tagged by category
2. **Weekly category totals** — once a week, read the app’s category or merchant totals and enter one number per budget category

Review the gap without a shame spiral. A red number is information: either the plan was unrealistic, a bill landed early, or spending needs a tweak next week. Move money between categories on purpose rather than pretending the plan was perfect.

A simple view that helps:

| Category | Planned (£) | Actual (£) | Left (£) |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| Groceries | 350 | 210 | 140 |
| Council tax | 160 | 160 | 0 |
| Eating out | 80 | 95 | −15 |

When “eating out” goes over, pull from a flexible category or cut next week’s plan — do not ignore it until payday.

## Annual and irregular costs

January and September feel expensive because annual bills cluster. Spread them with **sinking funds**: a monthly set-aside for a known future cost.

Common UK examples:

- Car MOT and service
- Car or home insurance renewals
- Christmas and birthdays
- Back-to-school
- Boiler cover or appliance replacement fund
- Holiday deposit

Work out the yearly cost, divide by 12 (or by the months until the bill), and treat that monthly amount as a planned line. When the bill arrives, the money is already waiting in savings or a labelled pot.

## Shared budgets (partners)

Google Sheets works well for couples because you can share **one live file** with edit access.

Agree a few ground rules so it stays calm:

- One person can own weekly data entry; the other still has full visibility
- Shared categories for joint bills; optional personal “fun money” lines so nobody feels policed
- A short Sunday check-in on the same screen rather than surprise screenshots mid-week

The sheet is a shared map, not a scorecard.

---

## A weekly 15-minute rhythm

Pick a fixed slot (Sunday evening or payday Monday works for many people).

In fifteen minutes:

1. Update actuals from the banking app
2. Move money between categories if something ran hot
3. Check which bills are due before next payday
4. Glance at sinking funds — still on track?
5. Note one change for next month if the same category keeps blowing

Consistency beats a perfect spreadsheet you never open.

## FAQ

### What categories should a UK budget include?

Start with rent or mortgage, council tax, energy, water, broadband, mobile, TV licence, groceries, transport, insurance, debt minimums, subscriptions, and at least one sinking fund for annual bills. Add or remove lines until the list matches your real statements.

### Can I budget in Google Sheets on my phone?

Yes. Install Google Sheets, open your copy from Drive, and update actuals on the go. Many people still prefer a laptop for the first setup, then use the phone for quick weekly edits.

### Should I use cash envelopes or categories?

Either can work. Digital “envelopes” are just categories with planned limits. Category budgeting in a sheet is usually easier with bank cards and standing orders; physical cash envelopes still suit people who want hard limits on spending money. Pick the system you will stick to.
